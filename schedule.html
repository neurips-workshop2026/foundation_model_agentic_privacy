Invited Talks

Aurélien Bellet

Post-Hoc Privacy Evaluation of Large Opaque Models: From Membership Inference to DP Auditing

Large-scale AI models are increasingly difficult to assess for privacy: their training data and pipelines are often inaccessible, and rigorous evaluation methods based on repeated retraining are computationally prohibitive. How can we nevertheless obtain statistically principled privacy assessments of such models?

I will present two complementary approaches to post-hoc privacy evaluation, both operating on fixed sets of known training members and non-members, without requiring access to the training process, retraining models, or controlled insertion of audit data. The first addresses population-level privacy evaluation through membership inference attacks (MIAs), while the second focuses on worst-case privacy leakage through differential privacy auditing, providing empirical lower bounds on privacy guarantees. Both approaches leverage ideas from causal inference to make post-hoc privacy evaluation more rigorous, addressing biases and confounding in the evaluation process and enabling meaningful assessments of opaque models from observational access alone.

Finally, I will introduce PANAME, an open-source privacy auditing library developed jointly by CNIL, ANSSI, PEReN, and Inria. Motivated by recent regulatory developments surrounding the application of GDPR to AI models, PANAME aims to translate research advances into operational tools for assessing models and supporting compliance-related analysis, thereby bridging the gap between methodological advances, regulatory expectations, and practical privacy assessment.

Pierre Fernandez

Watermarks and their radioactivity

We usually know little about the data that large models are trained on, which makes it hard to check whether someone’s content was used for training. This talk shows how watermarks can help. A watermark is a hidden signal added to images or text, and some of these signals are “radioactive”: a model trained on marked data keeps a trace of it. This was first shown for image classifiers (Sablayrolles et al., ICML 2020), then for diffusion models trained on watermarked images (Wang et al., ICLR 2024) and for language models trained on watermarked text (Gu et al., ICLR 2024). It can be used to detect model distillation (Sander et al., NeurIPS 2024), to prove that documents were used for pretraining (Wei et al., ACL Findings 2024), or to show that a dataset is used by a RAG system (Jovanovic et al., ICLR 2025). I will explain how these work, what they can tell us about model training data, and where they break.
