About PLOM

Foundation models, large language models, and agentic systems are rapidly reshaping the privacy landscape of machine learning. A common feature across these paradigms is their profound and intricate opacity: for a variety of reasons (including fierce commercial competition, the scale of training data, and confidentiality constraints), very little information about these systems is publicly disclosed. Large language models go through complex post-training and alignment pipelines on datasets crafted from human-AI interactions, and agentic systems introduce long-horizon data use, tool-mediated information flows, and data repurposing across contexts that users and institutions may not fully anticipate or overview. The lack of transparency of these systems, hence, creates new pathways for privacy failures arising from memorization, retrieval, tool use, memory, and cross-context information flow. At the same time, the assessment of these privacy risks is made increasingly difficult: researchers cannot examine the internal structure of these models, inspect the data, audit every step of the training pipeline, or retrain shadow models to evaluate the effect of privacy interventions. Together, these developments challenge existing definitions, benchmarks, mitigation strategies, governance frameworks, and legal notions of consent, purpose limitation, and accountability.

This workshop will provide a timely forum for examining memorization and privacy risks across opaque models, with particular emphasis on how these risks should be defined, measured, mitigated, and governed in real-world deployment settings. It will bring together researchers from privacy, machine learning, human-computer interaction (HCI), law, and agentic AI to examine a central question: what should privacy mean when increasingly opaque models are trained, adapted, deployed, and allowed to act on increasingly sensitive information? From a technical perspective, we need new definitions, evaluation strategies, threat models, mitigations, and formal guarantees for systems that may memorize training data and that can only be partially accessed by auditors. From a legal, policy, and deployment perspective, we must also ask: who is responsible when private information is exposed through a model output or an agent-to-agent interaction? What types of control mechanisms are available to each stakeholder within their respective scope of authority? How should privacy risks be disclosed to users and governed across the AI lifecycle? By connecting these technical and governance questions, the workshop aims to develop a shared research agenda for privacy in real-world AI deployments.

Our workshop will center on two complementary pillars: defining privacy risks in opaque models, and developing methods to evaluate, audit, and mitigate privacy failures in deployed foundation-model systems, including failures driven by memorization, retrieval, memory persistence, and tool-mediated information flow. We welcome submissions on evaluating and defining opacity, reverse engineering of large models, model-mediated disclosure, memorization, tool-mediated leakage, and failures that emerge from multi-step agent behavior rather than isolated model responses. We are especially interested in work that makes these risks measurable, including benchmarks, auditing tools, deployment studies, and open-source systems. By engaging researchers, regulators, applied scientists, and industry practitioners, the workshop will ground privacy threat models in realistic settings where AI systems interact with real user data. Our goal is to develop practical foundations for trustworthy and accountable AI deployment before unsafe privacy patterns become entrenched.
